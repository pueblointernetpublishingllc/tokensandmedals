So-called dollar price guide

Estimated values for 295 slabbed so-called dollars by Hibler-Kappen number and grade. Edition of November 2026. How we make these figures.

Our opinion, not a promise

These are Tokens and Medals' own estimates from a statistical formula. They are not appraisals, not offers to buy or sell, and not a prediction of what your piece will bring. Real prices at any venue can land well above or below them; about one sale in five lands outside the range shown. Do not rely on this guide alone to buy, sell, insure or value a piece for tax or estate purposes.

How to read it

  • The bold figure is our estimate for the piece in that grade as it would sell today at a major numismatic auction. The smaller figures are the range we expect about four sales in five to land in.
  • PCGS and NGC holders are priced separately: in the sales we studied, NGC-slabbed pieces brought about 23% less than the same piece and grade in a PCGS holder.
  • Weekly online auctions ran about 20% below the major floor auctions, and eBay about 20% below. Take that much off the figures for those venues.
  • Each piece lists the grades it exists in, within three points of a grade it has sold in. Further from the evidence we would rather say nothing.
  • Prices include the metal. For gold and silver pieces, check the melt value too; if the metal is worth more than our figure, the metal wins.

How we make these figures

The formula was built from public auction results of the major numismatic auction houses and from completed eBay sales of slabbed pieces. We studied those sales; none of them is reproduced here.

What goes in

  • Grade. Each grade point is worth about 10%.
  • Metal. Silver pieces bring about 1.9 times what the same kind of piece brings in copper or bronze, and gold about 3.3 times. The metal comes from the Hibler-Kappen catalogue or the grading service's own label, never from a guess.
  • Scarcity. How often the piece turned up for sale between 1970 and 2000. A piece seen twice as often brings about 22% less.
  • Age. When it was issued. Older issues bring more.
  • The holder (PCGS or NGC), the venue and the season. The holder is included because PCGS and NGC pieces plainly sell differently, even though it added little to the test scores; the venue and the season earned their place in the tests. Spring and summer sales ran about 16% below January-to-March ones; the guide averages over the year.
  • The date of the sale. Prices have drifted up about 3% a year. Every estimate is brought forward to today.
  • The piece's own sales. Each past sale is adjusted to today's grade, holder and venue, recent sales count for more, and the result is blended with what similar pieces bring, so one odd result can't set the price on its own.

What we tested and left out

Copper colour designations (BN, RB, RD), CAC stickers, plus grades, how many pieces have been slabbed, the number struck and diameter were all tried. None of them improved the predictions of sales the formula hadn't seen reliably enough to keep, so none is used. The guide does not price colour designations separately.

How well it works

We predicted every sale from 2016 on using only the sales that came before it. Half of those sales landed within 29% of our figure. For scale, even a perfect estimate would miss a typical single sale by about 22%: that is how much sales of the same piece in the same grade scatter around their typical price. The range caught 79% of the sales in that test, and 76% of the sales since 2024.

What isn't listed

Pieces with no sales we could study (the formula alone is too uncertain for those), restrikes, and grades below AU55. A piece missing from the guide is not a sign that it is common or rare.
