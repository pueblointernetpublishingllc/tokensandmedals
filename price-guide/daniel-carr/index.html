Daniel Carr · Moonlight Mint price guide

Estimated values for pieces from Daniel Carr's Moonlight Mint in Loveland, Colorado: 231 with sales of their own, and 1152 more, shown in blue, that have never sold and are estimated from the formula alone. Fantasy-date over-strikes, catalogue issues and a few pieces outside the catalogue. Edition of November 2026. How we make these figures.

Our opinion, not a promise

These are Tokens and Medals' own estimates from a statistical formula. They are not appraisals, not offers to buy or sell, and not a prediction of what your piece will bring. Real prices at any venue can land well above or below them; about one sale in five lands outside the range shown. Do not rely on this guide alone to buy, sell, insure or value a piece for tax or estate purposes. Tokens and Medals is not affiliated with Daniel Carr or Moonlight Mint.

Figures in blue, marked “formula only”, are extrapolations. Those pieces have never sold in the records we studied, so there is no price data for the piece itself: the figure comes only from what other pieces of the same issue or design, the same catalogue section and the same metal bring, and it is much less certain.

How to read it

  • The bold figure is our estimate as the piece would sell today on eBay at a fixed price. The smaller figures are the range we expect about four sales in five to land in.
  • 70 slab and 69 or lower are for graded pieces (most of the graded Carr pieces in the sales we studied were ANACS slabs); Raw is ungraded. A dash means we have no basis for that grade: for a piece with sales, it never sold in that grade; for a piece that has never sold, ANACS reports no slabs at that grade for the piece or its issue, so there is nothing to price.
  • Blue figures are formula-only estimates for pieces that have never sold. We tested the same method on 317 first-ever sales, each predicted before the piece had sold: half came within 38% of the figure, and 79% fell inside the range, which is much wider than for pieces with sales. Use the switch above the list to hide them.
  • Over-strikes are listed by design and fantasy date, following the maker's own over-strike list where the piece is on it; catalogue issues by the sections and numbers of the Moonlight Mint catalogue, where the maker gives mintages and issue prices.
  • The market for Carr's work is short on history: this edition rests mostly on sales from spring 2026 on. Treat new issues with extra care; their prices move fastest.

The formula was built from completed eBay sales of Carr's pieces, each matched to the maker's catalogue or over-strike list by matching rules that were then spot-checked against a random sample of sales. We studied those sales; none of them is reproduced here.

What goes in

  • Grade. A 70 slab brought about 19% more than a raw piece, and a slab of 69 or lower about 4% more.
  • Metal. Silver pieces brought about 1.6 times what base-metal pieces of the same kind did.
  • Sets and lots. They are priced by how many coins they hold: a five-coin set brought about 3.5 times a single piece of the same kind.
  • How it sold. Auctions ran about 14% below fixed-price sales. This one is in by design, because the guide is priced at fixed price.
  • The date of the sale. Carr prices eased about 0.8% a month on average across the period, and every estimate is brought forward to today.
  • The piece's own sales. Each is adjusted to the grade and date being priced, the middle of them is taken, and the result is blended with what similar pieces bring (the same issue or over-strike design, then the same catalogue section), so one odd result can't set the price on its own.

Pieces that have never sold

For a piece with no sales, the figure is the formula's value from what other pieces of the same issue or over-strike design bring, the catalogue section, the metal and the grade, with no adjustment from sales of its own. To check it, we went back through every month of sales and predicted each first-ever sale of a piece using only the months before it: 317 first-ever sales in all. Half landed within 38% of the figure, and the range shown caught 79% of them when tested on pieces it had not been set from. Pieces from issues that had never sold did not need a range of their own. The graded columns appear only where ANACS's population report shows slabs at that grade for the piece or its issue. For a never-sold silver piece whose weight the maker gives, the figure never falls below the value of its silver at $51.95 an ounce, the Chainlink price on 2026-11-07, when this edition was built; the figures do not yet follow the live metal price. Gold pieces that have never sold are left out, because their price follows their weight, which the formula does not model.

What we tested and left out

The grading service, proof finish, the maker's issue price and published mintage, whether a piece is an over-strike or a catalogue issue, a separate price trend for each issue, and ANACS population counts (how many pieces are graded 70, what share of graded pieces reached 70, and whether a piece sits at the top grade with only a few there) were all tried. Once grade, metal and the piece itself were known, none improved the predictions of sales the formula hadn't seen reliably enough to keep, so none is used.

How well it works

We predicted each month's sales using only the months before it. Half of those sales landed within 23% of our figure, and 80% of the most recent months' sales fell inside the range. In the latest months Carr prices came in about 15% under what the earlier months suggested; this edition includes those sales, and the next one will show whether the softening holds.

What isn't listed

Pieces we could not identify with confidence from the sale descriptions, mixed lots, sizes sold together under one name, the individual Lesher merchant imprints (the Lesher line covers them), issues the maker lists as never released or with none struck, and gold pieces that have never sold. A piece missing from the guide is not a sign that it is common or rare.
