Daniel Carr · Moonlight Mint price guide

Estimated values for pieces from Daniel Carr's Moonlight Mint in Loveland, Colorado: 231 with sales of their own, and 1423 more, shown in blue, that have no sales in the records we gathered and are estimated from the formula alone. Fantasy-date over-strikes, catalogue issues and a few pieces outside the catalogue. Edition of November 2026. How we make these figures.

Our opinion, not a promise

These are Tokens and Medals' own estimates from a statistical formula. They are not appraisals, not offers to buy or sell, and not a prediction of what your piece will bring. Real prices at any venue can land well above or below them; about one sale in five lands outside the range shown. Do not rely on this guide alone to buy, sell, insure or value a piece for tax or estate purposes. Tokens and Medals is not affiliated with Daniel Carr or Moonlight Mint.

Figures in blue, marked “formula only”, are extrapolations. We have no sales of those pieces in the records we gathered, so there is no price data for the piece itself: the figure comes only from what other pieces of the same issue or design, the same catalogue section and the same metal bring, and it is much less certain.

How to read it

  • The bold figure is our estimate as the piece would sell today on eBay at a fixed price. The smaller figures are the range we expect about four sales in five to land in.
  • 70 slab and 69 or lower are for graded pieces (most of the graded Carr pieces in the sales we studied were ANACS slabs); Raw is ungraded. A dash means we have no basis for that grade: for a piece with sales, none of its sales in our records was in that grade; for a piece with no sales in our records, ANACS reports no slabs at that grade for the piece or its issue, so there is nothing to price.
  • Catalogue numbers link to that number in the maker's catalogue (most browsers jump straight to it), fantasy dates link to the maker's write-up of the over-strike, and each section links to the maker's catalogue page and store. Where we matched a catalogue number to a product in the maker's store, a small “store” link beside the number opens that product. Pieces without sales in our records that the formula cannot tell apart, such as the finishes of one issue, share a line that lists all their numbers.
  • Pieces that contain gold, silver, platinum or palladium follow the metal: the line above the list shows the prices in use. With JavaScript on, they are live prices traded around the clock, weekends included: gold is the trading price of PAXG (a token backed by one troy ounce of gold, according to its issuer) on Uniswap, and silver, platinum and palladium are the prices of the perpetual futures on them on Hyperliquid. Where gold or silver cannot be read, or strays far from it, Chainlink's on-chain price feed is used instead; for platinum and palladium, the price when this edition was built.
  • Blue figures are formula-only estimates for pieces with no sales in our records. We tested the same method on 318 sales that were the first of their piece in our records, each predicted before that sale: half came within 37% of the figure, and 78% fell inside the range, which is much wider than for pieces with sales. Use the switch above the list to hide them.
  • Over-strikes are listed by design and fantasy date, following the maker's own over-strike list where the piece is on it; catalogue issues by the sections and numbers of the Moonlight Mint catalogue, where the maker gives mintages and issue prices.
  • The market for Carr's work is short on history: this edition rests mostly on sales from spring 2026 on. Treat new issues with extra care; their prices move fastest.

How we make these figures

The formula was built from completed eBay sales of Carr's pieces, each matched to the maker's catalogue or over-strike list by matching rules that were then spot-checked against a random sample of sales. We studied those sales; none of them is reproduced here.

What goes in

  • Grade. A 70 slab brought about 14% more than a raw piece, and a slab of 69 or lower about 4% more.
  • Precious metals. A piece that contains gold, silver, platinum or palladium is priced as its metal at today's price plus a premium. The metal content comes from the weight and fineness in the maker's catalogue, including the numbered notes under each table, such as an edge marked “ONE TROY OZ .999 SILVER” (where he gives a weight but no fineness, .999, his bullion standard, is assumed, and where an entry gives a range or more than one weight, the smallest is used); where the catalogue gives no weight, from the weight on his store page for that piece (the low end, where the store gives a range); or, for an over-strike, from the genuine coin it is struck on, such as the 0.7734 oz of silver in a Morgan or Peace dollar. Where neither states a weight, as for many 39mm silver pieces, the figure cannot include the metal and is not floored at it. Platinum and palladium are valued at the prices of Hyperliquid's platinum and palladium perpetual futures, live on the page like gold and silver; their prices on past sale dates come from an open daily price dataset, checked against the World Bank's monthly platinum average. The premium is what the formula learns from sales, each sale's metal valued at that metal's price on the day it sold. Beyond the metal, silver pieces carried a premium about 1.3 times that of base-metal pieces of the same kind.
  • Sets and lots. They are priced by how many coins they hold: a five-coin set brought about 3.0 times a single piece of the same kind. This one is in by design, because a set is a different thing from a single piece.
  • How it sold. Auctions ran about 20% below fixed-price sales. This one is in by design, because the guide is priced at fixed price.
  • The date of the sale. Once the metal is separated out, premiums moved about 0.4% a month on average across the period, and every estimate is brought forward to today.
  • The piece's own sales. Each is adjusted to the grade and date being priced, the middle of them is taken, and the result is blended with what similar pieces bring (the same issue or over-strike design, then the same catalogue section), so one odd result can't set the price on its own.

Why melt plus premium

We tested pricing pieces as metal plus premium against pricing the whole piece directly, on sales the formula had not seen. For pieces with sales of their own it was more accurate, for pieces with metal and without, and it is what the guide uses for them. For pieces with no sales in our records, pricing the whole piece directly tested slightly better, so those figures come from the formula directly and are simply never shown below the value of the metal they contain. Gold pieces with no sales in our records are the exception: a gold piece's value is mostly its gold, so its figure is its gold at today's price plus a premium, the way pieces with sales are priced. Our records hold very few sales of Carr gold pieces, too few to test these figures against, so treat them as the least certain in the guide.

Pieces with no sales in our records

For a piece with no sales in our records, the figure is the formula's value from what other pieces of the same issue or over-strike design bring, the catalogue section, the metal and the grade, with no adjustment from sales of its own. To check it, we went back through every month of sales and predicted each sale that was the first of its piece in our records, using only the months before it: 318 such sales in all. Half landed within 37% of the figure, and the range shown caught 78% of them when tested on pieces it had not been set from. Pieces from issues with no sales in our records did not need a range of their own. The graded columns appear only where ANACS's population report shows slabs at that grade for the piece or its issue. For a piece with no sales in our records that contains precious metal, the figure never falls below the value of that silver at the live price.

What we tested and left out

The grading service, proof finish, the maker's issue price and published mintage, whether a piece is an over-strike or a catalogue issue, a separate price trend for each issue, and ANACS population counts (how many pieces are graded 70, what share of graded pieces reached 70, and whether a piece sits at the top grade with only a few there) were all tried. Once grade, metal and the piece itself were known, none improved the predictions of sales the formula hadn't seen reliably enough to keep, so none is used.

How well it works

We predicted each month's sales using only the months before it. Half of those sales landed within 21% of our figure, and 80% of the most recent months' sales fell inside the range. In the latest months Carr prices came in about 12% under what the earlier months suggested; this edition includes those sales, and the next one will show whether the softening holds.

What isn't listed

Pieces we could not identify with confidence from the sale descriptions, mixed lots, sizes sold together under one name, the individual Lesher merchant imprints (the Lesher line covers them), and issues the maker lists as never released or with none struck. A piece missing from the guide is not a sign that it is common or rare.
